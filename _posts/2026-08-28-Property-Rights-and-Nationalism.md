---
title: Property Rights and Nationalism
description:  Property rights don't exist on their own but rely on the Nation-State to enforce them.
layout: post
---


Property is often spoken of as though it were a natural relationship between a person and a thing. I own a house, therefore the house is my property. I own a piece of land, therefore I have a right to it. I own a patent, therefore an invention belongs to me. But there is something hidden inside the simple word *own*: the expectation that everyone else will recognize the claim, and that someone will enforce it if they do not.

A person can possess a thing without a state. A man can build a shelter, cultivate a piece of land, or carry a tool, and others may recognize his possession through custom or social convention. But possession and property are not quite the same thing. Property, in the modern sense, is an institutional relationship. It is a socially recognized and enforceable claim to control a resource. The difference becomes especially important when possession is challenged. If someone takes my bicycle, my assertion that it is mine becomes meaningful because there exists a system of rules under which that claim can be adjudicated and, ultimately, enforced.

Modern property rights therefore rest on something that is easy to overlook: political authority.

The modern world is divided into sovereign jurisdictions. There is no single global institution that defines every property right and enforces every ownership claim. Instead, there are Indian property rights, American property rights, British property rights, Singaporean property rights, and so forth. The legal system of each jurisdiction determines what may be owned, who may own it, how ownership is transferred, what happens when an owner dies, what contracts are enforceable, and what remedies are available when property is taken or damaged.

This becomes even clearer when we move from ordinary physical property to the more sophisticated forms of wealth that dominate modern economies. A factory is a physical object, but the legal ownership of the factory is an institutional construct. A corporation can own that factory because a legal system recognizes corporations as entities capable of owning property. A shareholder does not directly own the factory; he owns a financial claim on the corporation. A bondholder owns a contractual claim on future payments. A patent holder does not physically possess an invention; the state gives the holder an enforceable right to prevent others from making, using, or selling the invention under specified conditions.

Patents make the point particularly starkly. There is nothing in nature that corresponds to a patent. A physical object can be possessed. Land can be occupied. But the claim that no one else may manufacture a particular invention is not a physical fact. It is a legal rule. Without an institution capable of defining and enforcing that rule, the patent simply ceases to exist as a property right. The same is true, in different ways, of copyrights, trademarks, corporate shares, mining concessions, spectrum licences, and many other forms of modern property.

Property, therefore, is not merely about what we possess. It is about what society agrees that we may exclusively control.

And in the modern world, "society" ultimately means political jurisdiction.

This does not mean that property was invented by the modern nation-state. Property norms are ancient. Human communities developed rules governing possession, inheritance, land use, and exchange long before the emergence of modern states. Medieval communities had property systems; tribes had customary systems; merchants developed private rules for trade across political boundaries. Human beings have always needed rules governing scarce resources.

The stronger and more interesting claim is therefore not that property requires the nation-state in some metaphysical sense. It is that **the modern system of property rights is inseparable from the system of sovereign states within which those rights are defined and enforced**.

This raises an interesting question: what exactly are property owners paying the state for?

The conventional answer is taxation: citizens pay taxes because governments need revenue to provide public services. But there is another way of looking at the relationship. The state provides a vast institutional infrastructure that makes private property possible. It maintains courts that enforce contracts, police that protect possession, registries that establish title, corporate laws that create legal entities, bankruptcy laws that determine competing claims, currencies in which obligations are denominated, and international agreements that allow property and contracts to function across borders.

In this sense, the state provides a kind of **property-rights protection service**.

A person does not merely own a house; he owns a house within a legal system that recognizes his title. A company does not merely possess a factory; it possesses a factory within a jurisdiction that recognizes its corporate existence and property claims. An investor does not merely own shares; she owns shares within a securities and corporate-law system that defines what those shares mean.

The value of many forms of property is therefore partly dependent upon the institutional system that protects them.

This observation does not imply that all taxation is simply a "protection fee." The analogy has limits. The state provides many services unrelated to property, and citizens who own little property also benefit from the political and legal system. Nor does the argument imply that the state creates the underlying wealth. A farmer creates value through cultivation; an entrepreneur creates value through organization; an inventor creates value through discovery; a worker creates value through labour. The state should not be credited with creating everything merely because it enforces the rules under which economic activity occurs.

But the institutional contribution becomes particularly important when we consider economic rents.

Imagine two individuals. One owns a bicycle worth ₹20,000. Another owns a piece of urban land worth ₹20 crore. Both possess property rights protected by the same legal and political system. Yet the economic value of their exclusive rights is radically different. More importantly, the value of the land may rise dramatically because a city grows around it, a railway station is constructed nearby, roads are improved, businesses arrive, and millions of other people invest in the surrounding community. The landowner receives the resulting increase in site value without having created the underlying land.

This is where the insight of Henry George becomes relevant.

George's argument was not that private property itself was illegitimate. His distinction was between wealth created through labour and capital and economic rent arising from the ownership of land. Land is different because it is not produced by its owner and cannot be reproduced when demand increases. Much of its value is therefore generated by nature and by society around it.

From this perspective, a land value tax can be understood not simply as a tax on an asset, but as a charge on the economic rent associated with an exclusive right over a scarce natural resource. The state protects the owner's exclusive claim, but the underlying increase in land value often reflects the activity of the wider community.

The same reasoning produces an interesting distinction among different kinds of property. A patent is an exclusive right created by law. Land is a naturally scarce resource whose exclusive ownership is legally protected. A factory is a produced asset created through investment. A brand is an intangible asset created through years of reputation-building. A share is a financial claim on the future income of a corporation. These may all be called "property," but they do not have the same origin and should not necessarily receive the same economic or tax treatment.

This also provides another way of thinking about monopoly. If a government grants one company an exclusive mining licence, the resulting advantage exists because the state has legally excluded competitors. If a company develops a highly successful brand, competitors remain free to compete but must persuade consumers to switch. If a farmer owns land in a rapidly growing city, the scarcity of the location creates rent even though the farmer may have no monopoly over agricultural production. These are three different economic phenomena hidden beneath the single word "property."

The political economy of property therefore has two sides. On one side is the individual's claim to the fruits of his labour, investment, invention, and enterprise. On the other is the institutional structure that defines and protects exclusive claims over resources. A serious theory of property must recognize both.

This leads to a broader proposition: **property is private in its ownership, but public in its enforcement**.

The property owner receives the benefits of exclusive control, but the exclusivity itself exists within a political order. The state does not merely stand outside the property system and occasionally intervene in it; it is one of the institutions that makes the modern property system possible in the first place.

This does not mean that property owners owe the state an unlimited payment for the privilege of ownership. Nor does it mean that every tax can be justified as a charge for protection. It does, however, suggest that the appropriate tax treatment of different forms of property cannot be determined merely by asking whether something is "owned." We should ask what is being owned, how the right was created, where its value comes from, what the state contributes to its enforcement, and whether the resulting return represents compensation for productive activity or economic rent.

The division of humanity into sovereign political communities is therefore not merely a background fact to the property system. It is part of the institutional architecture within which modern property exists.

Perhaps the most interesting question is not whether property should exist without the state. It is whether we have adequately distinguished the different things for which the state protects property—and whether the economic returns generated by those protections should always accrue entirely to the private owner.

That question takes us from property to rent, from rent to taxation, and ultimately to a much older idea that remains remarkably relevant today: **the distinction between what an individual creates and what an individual merely has the exclusive right to collect.**
